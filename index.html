1 · Kamay and Possession Island, 1770

Did Cook have the “Consent of the Natives”? Did he even land on the island?

Draft, pending consultation

What is agreed

  • The Endeavour lay in the bay Cook later called Botany Bay, on the south side of what is now Sydney, from 29 April to 6 May 1770 (Cook’s dates, in ship’s time). In the language of the people there the bay is Kamay.
  • Cook fired muskets at two men who opposed the landing. Some of the small shot struck one of them. The landing party took forty or fifty spears from the huts.
  • Four of those spears went to Trinity College, Cambridge. They were handed back to the La Perouse Aboriginal Community on 23 April 2024.
  • On 22 August 1770 (Cook’s date; Banks’s is the 21st) Cook recorded raising the colours on an island off the tip of Cape York and taking possession of the whole east coast. He named it Possession Island. Its older names include Bedanug, Bedhan Lag, Tuidin and Thunadha.
  • The Admiralty’s secret instructions of 1768 had told him to take possession “with the Consent of the Natives”, or, if the country was uninhabited, by setting up marks.

Admiralty secret instructions to Lieutenant Cook · 30 July 1768

“You are also with the Consent of the Natives to take Possession of Convenient Situations in the Country in the Name of the King of Great Britain: Or: if you find the Country uninhabited take Possession for his Majesty by setting up Proper Marks and Inscriptions, as first discoverers and possessors.”

Source. Secret Instructions to Lieutenant Cook, 30 July 1768. Documenting a Democracy, Museum of Australian Democracy.

James Cook

Commander of the Endeavour · kept the ship’s journal

Source. Captain Cook’s Journal during His First Voyage Round the World, ed. W. J. L. Wharton (London, 1893), chapter VIII. Possession entry, 22 August 1770 (Wharton, p. 312).

See the source (Wharton, Wikisource) ↗Cook’s handwritten journal (NLA) ↗Portrait (Wikimedia Commons) ↗

What did they say they saw? At Kamay: “… as soon as we put the boat in they again came to oppose us, upon which I fir’d a musquet between the 2, which had no other Effect than to make them retire back, where bundles of their darts lay, and one of them took up a stone and threw at us, which caused my firing a Second Musquet, load with small Shott; and altho’ some of the shott struck the man, yet it had no other effect than making him lay hold on a Target. Immediately after this we landed, which we had no sooner done than they throw’d 2 darts at us; this obliged me to fire a third shott, soon after which they both made off …”
On the island, 22 August: “From the appearance of the people we expected they would have opposed our landing; but as we approached the shore they all made off, and left us in peaceable possession of as much of the Island as served our purpose.” Then: “… notwithstanding I had in the Name of his Majesty taken possession of several places upon this Coast, I now once More hoisted English Colours, and in the Name of His Majesty King George the Third took possession of the whole Eastern coast from the above Lat. down to this place by the Name of New Wales, together with all the Bays, Harbours, Rivers, and Islands, situated upon the said Coast; after which we fired 3 Volleys of small Arms, which were answer’d by the like number from the Ship.”

Could they really have seen it? He was in the boat at Kamay and says he was ashore on the island. He could see the two men on the rocks and the smokes on the islands. He could not understand anything the people said, so he could not know what they thought of the visitors or of the flag.

Why might they say it? He was writing for the Admiralty, whose instructions told him to take possession “with the Consent of the Natives”, or, if the country was uninhabited, by setting up marks. A journal for his superiors is also a record of how he carried out his orders.

Joseph Banks

Naturalist on the Endeavour · kept his own journal

Source. Joseph Banks, The Endeavour Journal of Sir Joseph Banks, April and August 1770 (Wikisource transcription, after the State Library of New South Wales manuscript).

See the source: April 1770 (Wikisource) ↗August 1770 (Wikisource) ↗Portrait (Wikimedia Commons) ↗

What did they say they saw? At Kamay: “They calld to us very loud in a harsh sounding Language of which neither us or Tupia understood a word, shaking their lances and menacing, in all appearance resolvd to dispute our landing to the utmost tho they were but two and we 30 or 40 at least. … We however thought it no improper measure to take away with us all the lances which we could find about the houses, amounting in number to forty or fifty.”
At the island, 21 August: “… we saw 10 Indians standing on a hill; 9 were armed with lances as we had been usd to see them, the tenth had a bow and arrows; 2 had also large ornaments of mother of Pearl shell hung round their necks. … The 3 Indians plac’d themselves upon the beach opposite to us as if resolvd either to oppose or assist our landing; when however we came about Musquet shot from them they all walkd leisurely away.” (“Musquet shot” means the distance a musket could reach.)

Could they really have seen it? He was in the boats at Kamay and went ashore at the island with Cook. He was a naturalist, not in command. He dates the island landing 21 August; Cook, keeping ship’s time, dates it the 22nd.

Why might they say it? His journal was his own record, not a report to the Admiralty. He was on the voyage to collect plants, animals and objects to study in England.

Kaurareg and Gudang Yadhaykenu accounts

Families of the island · recorded by the National Museum of Australia

Source. National Museum of Australia, Endeavour Voyage: “Bedanug, Thunadha, Bedhan Lag, Tuidin – Possession Island”.

See the source (National Museum of Australia) ↗Kaurareg native title judgment, 2001 (AustLII) ↗

What did they say they saw? Seriat Young, Kaurareg: “When you hear it from the elderly people, as soon as you mention Cook, they’ll growl and say, ‘Ah, that bloody no good Cook. He never even set foot on the bloody island!’”
Colina Wymarra, Gudang: “The story that my dad told me is when Cook sailed through the Torres Strait he put a flag on Possession Island. The Gudang are seafaring people and they often travel to the island and they saw the ‘cloth on a stick’ stuck in the sand on the beach.”
A Kaurareg man, Waubin Richard Aken, told the museum that Cook’s journal entries were used in the native title case “to help our argument and demonstrate that our people were seen on the island way back then.”

Could they really have seen it? These are family accounts, passed down for more than two hundred years and recorded for a national museum exhibition in 2020. The museum says some of the families’ ancestors were on the island on the day Cook landed.

Why might they say it? It is their Country. The accounts tell the story from the shore, which neither journal could do. In 2001 the Federal Court recognised Kaurareg native title over seven islands in the group.

Why it still matters

Cook’s claim on the island became part of the British case for possession of the east coast. More than two hundred years later, his journal was used as evidence that Kaurareg people were on the islands in 1770, in the case that recognised their native title in 2001. The spears taken at Kamay went back to the La Perouse community in 2024. The families of the island still tell their own account of the day.

Super Sleuths (optional): two dates, two names, one piece of advice

21 or 22 August? Banks dates the landing 21 August. Cook, keeping ship’s time, in which a day began at noon, enters it under the 22nd. At Kamay, Cook’s “29 April” was the afternoon of the 28th by the ordinary calendar. Why might two people on the same ship give different dates?

New Wales or New South Wales? Cook’s own journal, as Wharton printed it, says “New Wales”. Wharton notes that the Admiralty’s copy and the King’s copy say “New South Wales”.

James Douglas, Earl of Morton · “Hints” for Cook, Banks and Solander · August 1768

2 · Botany Bay and Warrane, 1788

What were the newcomers told to do about the people already there, and what do the first accounts say happened?

Draft, pending consultation

What is agreed

  • On 18 August 1786 Lord Sydney, the Home Secretary, wrote to the Treasury asking for ships to carry 750 convicts to Botany Bay.
  • Phillip’s instructions were dated 25 April 1787. The fleet sailed on 13 May 1787.
  • Phillip reached Botany Bay in the Supply on 18 January 1788. The rest of the fleet arrived on 19 and 20 January.
  • Phillip found the bay unsuitable and chose a cove in Port Jackson with fresh water. He named it Sydney Cove, after Lord Sydney. In the language of the Gadigal people, whose Country it is, the cove is Warrane.
  • On 26 January 1788 the colours were raised at Sydney Cove.

The Voyage of Governor Phillip to Botany Bay · 1789

“In the evening of the 26th the colours were displayed on shore, and the Governor, with several of his principal officers and others, assembled round the flag-staff, drank the king’s health, and success to the settlement …”

Governor Phillip’s Instructions

Issued in the name of King George III · 25 April 1787

Source. Instructions for Arthur Phillip, 25 April 1787. Transcript: Documenting a Democracy, Museum of Australian Democracy. The transcript prints “Savages Natives” as written.

See the source (transcript, Museum of Australian Democracy) ↗

What did they say they saw? “You are to endeavour by every possible means to open an Intercourse with the Savages Natives and to conciliate their affections, enjoining all Our Subjects to live in amity and kindness with them. And if any of Our Subjects shall wantonly destroy them, or give them any unnecessary Interruption in the exercise of their several occupations. It is our Will and Pleasure that you do cause such offenders to be brought to punishment according to the degree of the Offence.” The same instructions told him that on landing he was first to secure the settlement “as much as possible from any attacks or Interruptions of the Natives of that Country”.

Could they really have seen it? No. It was written in London, nine months before the fleet arrived. It is a witness to what the government ordered, not to what happened.

Why might they say it? It set out the King’s orders to his governor. It was meant to be obeyed, and it is the standard Phillip could later be judged by.

Arthur Phillip

First governor · commander of the fleet

Source. The Voyage of Governor Phillip to Botany Bay (London: John Stockdale, 1789), chapters VI and VIII. The title page says it was “compiled from Authentic Papers”.

See the source (Project Gutenberg) ↗Portrait (Wikimedia Commons) ↗

What did they say they saw? “At the very first landing of Governor Phillip on the shore of Botany Bay, an interview with the natives took place. They were all armed, but on seeing the Governor approach with signs of friendship, alone and unarmed, they readily returned his confidence by laying down their weapons.”
Writing of the weeks after the landing, the same book says: “Their dislike to the Europeans is probably increased by discovering that they intend to remain among them, and that they interfere with them in some of their best fishing places, which doubtless are, in their circumstances, objects of very great importance.”

Could they really have seen it? He was at the first landing at Botany Bay on 18 January 1788. The book itself was put together in London by its publisher from official papers, and printed under Phillip’s name while he was still in the colony.

Why might they say it? An account published under the governor’s name, the year after the landing, shows the expedition in a good light. The same book also records the people’s dislike of the newcomers, and one reason for it.

Watkin Tench

Captain-lieutenant of marines · wrote two books on the colony

Source. Watkin Tench, A Narrative of the Expedition to Botany Bay (London, 1789), chapter VIII.

See the source (Project Gutenberg) ↗

What did they say they saw? “For on the Supply’s arrival in the Bay on the 18th of the month, they were assembled on the beach of the south shore, to the number of not less than forty persons, shouting and making many uncouth signs and gestures. … the Governor immediately proceeded to land on that side, in order to take possession of his new territory, and bring about an intercourse between its old and new masters.”

Could they really have seen it? Not the first landing. His ship anchored on 20 January, two days later, so he wrote that part from what others told him. He went ashore himself three days after the first landing and describes meeting about a dozen men on the south side of the bay.

Why might they say it? He wrote a book for readers in England, published in 1789. His phrase for the landing is “take possession of his new territory”.

Why it still matters

26 January is Australia’s national day. Many Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander people mark it as a day of mourning, and on 26 January 1938 the Day of Mourning was held in Sydney. In 1992 the High Court, in Mabo v Queensland (No 2), rejected the idea that the land had belonged to no one. The instructions of 1787 and the accounts of 1788 are part of that argument.

The Voyage and Tench both describe the first landing at Botany Bay. Tench arrived two days later. The Voyage was compiled in London. Who, if anyone, among these witnesses saw the first landing with their own eyes?

3 · Smallpox and Arabanoo, 1789

What happened in the harbour in 1789, and where did the disease come from?

Draft, pending consultation

What is agreed

  • On 31 December 1788, on Phillip’s orders, a man was seized at Manly Cove so that the settlers could learn the language. In February 1789 he told them his name: Arabanoo.
  • In April 1789 boats began finding bodies in the coves and on the beaches of the harbour. The disease was smallpox.
  • A boy, Nanbaree, and a girl, whom Collins called Boo-roong and Tench called Abaroo, were brought in sick and recovered.
  • Arabanoo nursed the sick, caught the disease and died on 18 May 1789.
  • No one in the settlement is recorded as catching the disease except one man, a North American sailor from the Supply, who died.

David Collins · on the name of the disease · April 1789

“Whether it had ever appeared among them before could not be discovered, either from him or from the children; but it was certain that they gave it a name (gal-gal-la); a circumstance which seemed to indicate a preacquaintance with it.”

Source. David Collins, An Account of the English Colony in New South Wales, vol. 1 (London, 1798), April 1789.

David Collins

Judge-advocate and secretary to the governor · in the colony 1788–1796

Source. David Collins, An Account of the English Colony in New South Wales, vol. 1 (London, 1798), April 1789 and the account of the natives.

See the source (Project Gutenberg) ↗

What did they say they saw? “Early in the month, and throughout its continuance, the people whose business called them down the harbour daily reported, that they found, either in excavations of the rock, or lying upon the beaches and points of the different coves which they had been in, the bodies of many of the wretched natives of this country. The cause of this mortality remained unknown until a family was brought up, and the disorder pronounced to have been the smallpox.”
Of a boat trip down the harbour with Arabanoo: “He looked anxiously around him in the different coves we visited; not a vestige on the sand was to be found of human foot … He lifted up his hands and eyes in silent agony for some time; at last he exclaimed, ‘All dead! all dead!’ and then hung his head in mournful silence …”

Could they really have seen it? He was in the settlement in April 1789, and he writes as one of the party in the boat (“the different coves we visited”). He does not give the date of the trip. He published his account in London in 1798, nine years later.

Why might they say it? He was the colony’s legal officer and kept a record of its years. He also wrote that the people had a name for the disease, gal-gal-la, which he took as a sign they had known it before.

Arthur Phillip

Governor · report to Lord Sydney, 13 February 1790

Source. Governor Phillip to Lord Sydney, 13 February 1790, State Library of New South Wales. Text as published by the National Portrait Gallery, Canberra.

See the source (National Portrait Gallery) ↗

What did they say they saw? “It is not possible to determine the number of natives who were carried off by this fatal disorder; it must be great. And judging from the information of the native now living with us, and who had recovered from the disorder before he was taken, one half of those who inhabit this part of the country died.”

Could they really have seen it? He saw the sick brought in to the settlement. The figure of “one half” is not his own count: he says it comes from “the native now living with us”, a man who had recovered from the disease before he was taken.

Why might they say it? This was an official report to the minister in London, written nearly a year after the outbreak. A governor’s report has to explain what happened in his colony.

The competing explanations

From Tench in 1793 to historians and a museum today

Source. Watkin Tench, A Complete Account of the Settlement at Port Jackson (London, 1793), chapter IV, footnote; Judy Campbell, Invisible Invaders (2002); Craig Mear, Journal of the Royal Australian Historical Society 94 (2008); National Museum of Australia, “Smallpox epidemic”.

Tench (Project Gutenberg) ↗National Museum of Australia ↗

What did they say they saw? Tench’s footnote asks: “… is it a disease indigenous to the country? Did the French ships under Monsieur de Peyrouse introduce it? … Had it travelled across the continent from its western shore, where Dampier and other European voyagers had formerly landed? Was it introduced by Mr. Cook? Did we give it birth here? No person among us had been afflicted with the disorder since we had quitted the Cape of Good Hope, seventeen months before. It is true, that our surgeons had brought out variolous matter in bottles; but to infer that it was produced from this cause were a supposition so wild as to be unworthy of consideration.”
Judy Campbell (2002) argued that smallpox reached the north coast with Macassan trepang fishermen from Sulawesi and travelled south along Aboriginal trade routes. Craig Mear (2008) argued that it came from the First Fleet, with the surgeons’ bottles as a possible source. The National Museum of Australia calls the bottles “a more likely source”, and says how they could have infected anyone “is unknown”.

Could they really have seen it? None of them saw the disease arrive. Tench was in the settlement in 1789. The historians and the museum wrote more than two hundred years later, from the written records.

Why might they say it? Each explanation changes the story of how the colony began. Notice where each one starts, and what each one would need to be true.

Why it still matters

Phillip reported that about half the people around the harbour died. Whether the disease came from the north, from the First Fleet, or by some other route is still argued by historians and by the National Museum. The answer changes how the first years of the colony are told.

Super Sleuths (optional): Tench’s questions

Tench, writing in 1793, lists possible origins: the country itself, the French ships, the west coast, Cook, the settlers, the surgeons’ bottles. Which does he dismiss, and what reason does he give? Which do the later historians take up?

Phillip put Arabanoo’s age at about twenty-four. Tench thought him about thirty. What does a difference like that tell you about the people writing?

4 · Bennelong, Barangaroo and Pemulwuy, 1789–1802

What do the records show about the people who dealt with the colony, and those who fought it?

Draft, pending consultation

What is agreed

  • Late in 1789 two men, Bennelong and Colbee, were seized and brought to Sydney Cove. Colbee escaped within about a week. Bennelong escaped in May 1790.
  • On 7 September 1790 Phillip was speared at Manly Cove by a man later named as Wileemarin. He recovered.
  • Bennelong later returned to the settlement. A brick hut was built for him on the eastern point of Sydney Cove, now Bennelong Point. Barangaroo came with him. She died not long after giving birth to a daughter, Dilboong.
  • In December 1790 John McEntire, the governor’s gamekeeper, was speared. Men from the harbour named the man responsible as Pemulwuy, a Bidjigal man. Phillip sent out an armed expedition. It found no one.
  • Pemulwuy led resistance around Parramatta, Toongabbie and Prospect Hill through the 1790s. In November 1801 Governor King ordered that he be brought in, dead or alive. He was shot dead in 1802, and his head was sent to England.

What did they say they saw? At Manly Cove, 7 September 1790: “To remove his fear, governor Phillip threw down a dirk, which he wore at his side. The other, alarmed at the rattle of the dirk, and probably misconstruing the action, instantly fixed his lance in his throwing-stick. … the Indian, stepping back with one foot, aimed his lance with such force and dexterity, that striking the governor’s right shoulder, just above the collar-bone, the point glancing downward, came out at his back …”
Two men from Rose Hill, later that month, “expressed great dissatisfaction at the number of white men who had settled in their former territories.”
At a flogging in May 1791: “There was not one of them that did not testify strong abhorrence of the punishment and equal sympathy with the sufferer. The women were particularly affected; Daringa shed tears, and Barangaroo, kindling into anger, snatched a stick and menaced the executioner.”
The orders he was given in December 1790: “… we were, if practicable, to bring away two natives as prisoners; and to put to death ten; that we were to destroy all weapons of war but nothing else; that no hut was to be burned; that all women and children were to remain uninjured … That we were to cut off and bring in the heads of the slain; for which purpose hatchets and bags would be furnished.”

Could they really have seen it? He was not in the boat at Manly Cove; Phillip, Collins and Waterhouse were. He was in the settlement at the time of the flogging. He commanded the expedition of December 1790 himself and wrote down the orders he was given.

Why might they say it? His book was published in London in 1793, after he had left. He records Phillip’s orders, and also that the expedition found no one.

David Collins

Judge-advocate · left the colony in September 1796

What did they say they saw? At Barangaroo’s death: “When Ba-rang-a-roo Da-ring-ha, Bennillong’s wife, died, he determined at once to burn her, and requested Governor Phillip, Mr. White, and myself, to attend him.”
At Parramatta, March 1797: “… they intended, if possible, to seize upon Pe-mul-wy; who, in a great rage, threatened to spear the first man that dared to approach him, and actually did throw a spear at one of the soldiers. The conflict was now begun; a musket was immediately levelled at the principal, which severely wounded him. … Pe-mul-wy, who had received seven buck shot in his head and different parts of his body, was taken extremely ill to the hospital.”

Could they really have seen it? He attended Barangaroo’s funeral at Bennelong’s request. He was not in the colony in 1797: his first volume ends when he sailed in the Britannia in September 1796. His second volume, published in 1802, covers the years after he left, so that part comes from what others reported.

Why might they say it? He was writing a record of the colony for readers in Britain. His second volume is a witness to what was reported to him, not to what he saw.

Philip Gidley King

Governor of New South Wales from September 1800

Source. Historical Records of New South Wales, vol. 4 (Sydney, 1896): King to Lord Hobart, 30 October 1802, pp. 867–868. King to Sir Joseph Banks, 5 June 1802, Papers of Sir Joseph Banks, State Library of New South Wales, as quoted in the Australian Dictionary of Biography.

See the source (HRNSW vol. 4, Internet Archive) ↗Australian Dictionary of Biography ↗Portrait (Wikimedia Commons) ↗

What did they say they saw? To Lord Hobart: “I gave orders for every person doing their utmost to bring Pemulwye in either dead or alive … Some time after two settlers, not having the means of securing the persons of Pemulwye and another native, shot them. On this event they requested that Pemulwye’s head might be carried to the Governor …”
To Sir Joseph Banks: “… altho a terrible pest to the Colony, he was a Brave and independent character, understanding that the possession of a New Hollander’s Head is among the desiderata I have put it in spirits and forward it by the Speedy.”

Could they really have seen it? No. He was in Sydney, and reports what he was told about the shooting. He had the head, which he sent to England.

Why might they say it? He was writing to his minister, explaining his orders, and to Banks, who had been the naturalist at Kamay in 1770. The two letters describe the same man in different ways.

Why it still matters

Bennelong Point, where his hut stood, is now the site of the Sydney Opera House. Barangaroo’s name is on a part of the Sydney waterfront. According to the Australian Dictionary of Biography, Pemulwuy’s head “has not been found in an English repository to date.”

Super Sleuths (optional): two orders, two letters, two dates

17 or 22 November? The order of 17 November 1801 offers rewards for three convicts. The order of 22 November names Pemulwoy. Some modern accounts give the 17 November rewards as the price on Pemulwuy. Look at both orders in the Historical Records and decide which one names whom.

One governor, two letters. King wrote to Banks in June 1802 and to Lord Hobart in October 1802. Compare how he describes Pemulwuy in each.

9 or 10 December? Tench dates McEntire’s spearing 9 December 1790. Collins dates it the 10th.

Conflicts to flag.

  • Who shot Pemulwuy: King’s report to Hobart says “two settlers”; the Australian Dictionary of Biography names Henry Hacking, a seaman of the First Fleet; the historian Doug Kohlhoff has argued for settlers from around Parramatta, Toongabbie and Prospect Hill. Date: on or just before 2 June 1802.
  • McEntire’s spearing: 9 December 1790 (Tench) or 10 December (Collins). He died on 20 January 1791.
  • The orders of 17 and 22 November 1801 (HRNSW vol. 4, pp. 626–629): the reward scale belongs to the 17 November order about three outlawed convicts; the 22 November order names Pemulwoy. Some secondary accounts attach the reward scale to Pemulwuy directly.
  • King to Banks, 5 June 1802, is quoted from the Australian Dictionary of Biography and has not been checked against the State Library manuscript; spellings vary between published versions.
  • Lord Hobart’s dispatch is quoted from the National Museum of Australia.
  • That the head went to the Royal College of Surgeons museum rests on secondary reporting.
  • Collins’s second volume (1802) covers events after he left the colony in September 1796.
